# A cone-opponent amplitude budget for imperceptible color vibration on head-mounted displays

Code and condition-level data for the paper. Everything here runs from a clean
checkout and regenerates all ten figures exactly as they appear in the
manuscript.

```
pip install -r requirements.txt
python make_figures.py            # ten PDFs and PNGs into out/
python make_figures.py --loci     # re-solve the stimulus construction first
jupyter lab tutorial.ipynb        # the same thing, explained step by step
```

## What color vibration is, and what this repository computes

A color-vibration stimulus alternates two colors fast enough that the eye fuses
them into their time average, while a camera sampling faster than the eye still
resolves the two separately. The amplitude of that alternation is what decides
whether a viewer notices it, and the amplitude a viewer tolerates turns out to
depend strongly on the direction the alternation takes in cone-opponent space.

Two things live here. The first is the **stimulus construction**: given a
MacAdam ellipse and a radius, solve for the pair of display colors that sits at
that radius along the equal-chroma direction under CAM16 with the
Helmholtz–Kohlrausch correction, and is cone-isoluminant. The second is the
**figure pipeline** that turns the measured detection rates into the figures of
the paper.

## Layout

```
code/
  share/cfvi.py              linear sRGB -> XYZ -> LMS, and the cone-opponent
                             decomposition a_Lum, a_RG, a_S of a color pair
  codes/cfvi.py              the same conversions plus synth(), which builds a
                             pair from the three amplitudes; run it directly for
                             a self-check against a pair quoted in the paper
  codes/macadam_newton.py            MacAdam ellipse geometry in the CIE 1960 uv plane
  codes/macadam_newton_cam16hk.py    the CAM16 + Helmholtz-Kohlrausch stage
  chromagazer/macadam_newton.py      the ellipse geometry the figures import

data/
  conditions.json            one entry per experimental condition: the two
                             linear display values it alternated, the trial
                             counts pooled over observers, and, for system A, a
                             95% cluster-bootstrap interval over observers
  results_data.json          detection rate per condition, by system
  axes_fit.json              coefficients of the fitted model, and the three
                             measured series with their bootstrap intervals
  fine_loci.json             the solved pair chromaticities below the smallest
                             measured radius; regenerated by figures/fine_loci.py

figures/                     one script per figure; see the table below
images/                      screenshots taken during the experiment
out/                         generated figures land here
make_figures.py              runs the lot
```

## Start here: `tutorial.ipynb`

[`tutorial.ipynb`](tutorial.ipynb) walks through the whole thing and is the
easiest way in. It is committed with its output, so it reads on GitHub without
running anything.

- what the condition table holds, and how a pair of display colors becomes the
  three cone-opponent amplitudes
- the stimulus construction solved step by step for one ellipse and one radius,
  then checked against the value the experiment actually presented
- the visibility index, and where the thresholds and their ratios come from
- each of the ten figures, generated and shown, with what it says

|---|---|---|
| 1 `fig_concept` | `gen_fig1g.py` | ellipse geometry, fitted coefficients |
| 2 `fig_xy` | `gen_xy3.py` | `conditions.json`, `fine_loci.json` |
| 3 `fig_design` | `gen_design3.py` | `conditions.json` |
| 4 `fig_stimulus` | `gen_stimulus.py` | `images/` |
| 5 `fig_rq1`, 9 `fig_pred` | `gen_rq1_pred.py` | `conditions.json` |
| 6 `fig_rq2`, 8 `fig_rq4` | `gen_grids.py` | `results_data.json` |
| 7 `fig_axes` | `gen_axes.py` | `axes_fit.json` |
| 10 `fig_budget` | `gen_budget.py` | ellipse geometry, fitted coefficients |

`figures/fine_loci.py` is the stimulus construction itself. It solves the
four-step construction at every integer radius, and checks the solution against
the values the presentation software actually recorded: the worst disagreement
over all measured conditions is 0.0015 in xy, which is within the 8-bit
quantization of the framebuffer. It takes a few minutes and writes
`data/fine_loci.json`.

## What is not here

**The per-trial responses are not included.** The trial logs record, for each
observer, every stimulus presented and whether flicker was reported. Consent for
the study did not extend to publishing data at that level, so what is published
is the condition level: for each condition, how many trials were run over all
observers and on how many of them flicker was reported. Two figures depend on
quantities that can only be computed from the per-trial data — the maximum
likelihood fit of the mixed model, and the cluster bootstrap over observers.
Those results are included as numbers, in `axes_fit.json` and in the `ci95`
field of `conditions.json`, so the figures reproduce; the fitting itself does
not run from this repository.

Data underlying the results presented in the paper are not publicly available at
this time, for reasons of participant privacy, but may be obtained from the
authors upon reasonable request.

## Reproducing the figures exactly

Every figure in `out/` is pixel-identical to the one in the manuscript. Figures
are drawn at their printed size (5.25 in wide, the text width of the Optica
template) with 8 pt type, and are placed in the paper without scaling.

## Requirements

Python 3.11 with the packages in `requirements.txt`. `colour-science` is needed
by the chromaticity diagram in Fig. 2 and by the CAM16 stage.
